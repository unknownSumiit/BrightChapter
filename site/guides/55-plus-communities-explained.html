55+ Communities Explained: What to Know Before You Buy

By YOUR NAME · Updated November 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Active adult communities, often called 55+ communities, are a popular choice for people who want a low-maintenance home and an active social life. Here is how they work and what to check before you buy.

What is a 55+ community?

A 55+ community is a neighborhood or building designed for older adults. Under the federal Housing for Older Persons Act (HOPA), a community can qualify as “housing for older persons” if at least 80 percent of occupied units have at least one resident aged 55 or older, it publishes and follows policies showing that intent, and it verifies residents’ ages. Each community sets its own detailed rules within the law.

What makes them appealing

  • Low maintenance: The HOA often handles lawn care, snow removal, and exterior upkeep.
  • Amenities: Clubhouses, pools, fitness centers, walking paths, and planned activities.
  • Social connection: Neighbors at a similar stage of life, with clubs and events.
  • Thoughtful design: Many homes are single-level with wider doorways and fewer stairs.

Questions to ask before you buy

  • What are the age rules? Can younger spouses or adult children live with you? How long can grandchildren visit?
  • What do the HOA fees cover, how much have they increased in recent years, and how healthy is the reserve fund?
  • Are there any planned special assessments?
  • What are the rules about rentals, pets, parking, exterior changes, and guests?
  • How far is it to doctors, hospitals, shopping, and your family?
  • Is the home easy to resell? Ask your agent how quickly homes in the community have sold recently.

Read the documents

Before you buy, you should receive the community’s governing documents, often called the CC&Rs, bylaws, and rules, plus financial statements. Read them carefully, or have a real estate attorney review them with you.

55+ community or senior living?

55+ communities are for independent living, and you own your home. They do not usually provide meals, health care, or personal care. If you expect to need more support, you may want to compare independent living, assisted living, or continuing care retirement communities (CCRCs).
